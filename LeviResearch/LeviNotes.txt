    The Release of Prisoners of War from Britain in 1813 and 1814 
	The decisive defeat of Napoleon at the Battle of Leipzig in 1813 convinced many of his German allies to throw in their lot with the Coalition forces.

	With the large numbers of Germans and Italians in the prison depots who were being conscripted into the forces arrayed against Napoleon, this was seen as potentially a serious problem.	

	There were already such nationalities being held at Valleyfield, so by August 1812 there were at least 641 Italians and 588 Germans collected at the depot.

	The only choice these men were given was that of which force they could join. From Valleyfield they enlisted into the King’s German Legion, the 1st Dutch Regiment and most of the Italians into the army of the King of Sardinia.
	
	British Prison Depots: Dartmoor, Plymouth, Stapleton, Norman Cross, Chatham, Portchester, Portsmouth, Valleyfield, Perth

        The first entry for him is the most interesting - he is noted as being present from 16th December 1813 - and his entry is annotated 'vol from Pris of War;' together with the 2 men below him [and many others in the ledger]. I'm pretty sure the bracket includes him as well as the 2 following men, but I don't know what the comment 'as no 5' against all 3 of them means.  I could possibly investigate this further by taking another look at the paylists.  In subsequent paylists he appears as 'Leven Demell', 'Luen Demi', 'Lewin Demel', 'Leven Demell', 'Lew. Demel',. Here Leven is annotated with a check mark with 2 lines through it; Notes on the paylist say that 'men checked out thus ..are charged sub[sequentl]y in 3d B[atallio]n 60th per Return voucher 6 of P[ay] L[ist] to Sep 17 of that Bn'.

(p. 102)"The Germans of the 60th, or those who remained, were not ready until the second grant was made up in 1821.  The grant of six thousand nine hundred acres, which went into effect in January, 1822, happily names the corps to which each man formerly belonged.  Of the forty-five grantees, twenty-four had been in the Nova Scotia Fencibles (two of them had appeared on the previous grant), four in the Royal Artillery, two in the Newfoundland Fencibles, and one in the 76th. From the foregoing, it would appear that 109 soldiers were confirmed in the possession of the nineteen thousand nine hundred acres in Sherbrooke."

The Royal Newfoundland Fencibles were disbanded at Halifax in June 1816. Two captains, five lieutenants, one ensign, one quarter-master, seven sergeants, and eighty-three of the rank and file, 99 in all, accepted the offer of free land in Nova Scotia. Most of them went to the new military settlement laid out for them at Sherwood and Sherbrooke (now New Ross) in Lunenburg County.
The Nova Scotia Fencibles were disbanded at Halifax in July, 1816. Led by Captain William Ross, 172 of them are said to have arrived at Sherbrooke on August 7, 1816


J.S. Martell, op. cit. p.93 ff
More disbanded soldiers were placed along the projected Halifax-Annapolis Road in 1817. In April of the year, Surveyor-General Morris wrote to Captain Ross of Sherbrooke: "You may soon expect —Many Setlers [sic] Germans and Highlanders on your Road between Gold River and Annapolis . . . " In the same month Dalhousie asked Lord Bathurst for permission (received) to grant free land to about 50 Germans of the 7th Batt., 60th Regiment. Another military settlement on this road, Dalhousie, seems to have been started late in 1817.
